The Hellings–Downs curve, live

In 2023, astronomers found that pairs of pulsars across the sky wiggle in a subtly coordinated way — the fingerprint of gravitational waves rippling through our galaxy. How coordinated depends only on the angle between the two pulsars on the sky. Pick two pulsars on the map (or drag the slider) and watch where they land on the famous curve.

3. Where it lands on the curve

The black line is the exact Hellings–Downs prediction Γ(θ) = ½ − x/4 + (3/2)·x·ln x, with x = (1 − cos θ)/2. The red dot is your pair. Drag the dot, the slider, or the map — they stay in sync.

Compare with other causes:

Why is this particular shape the smoking gun for gravitational waves? Because the leading rival explanations would leave different fingerprints. A drifting reference clock would nudge every pulsar pair by the same amount, whatever their separation — a flat monopole. An error in the Solar System’s ephemeris (a mis-placed barycentre) would imprint a dipole that varies as cos θ. Only an isotropic, general-relativistic gravitational-wave background produces the quadrupolar Hellings–Downs shape drawn here — which is exactly why PTA collaborations fit for all three multipoles at once and report the quadrupole as dominant. Tick the boxes above to overlay the monopole and dipole reference shapes and see the contrast (both are illustrative shapes, anchored to the curve at θ = 0).

4. Now watch the actual wiggles

The curve above is the correlation averaged over many sources. Here is the other half of the story, in the time domain: drop a single supermassive black-hole binary somewhere on the sky and watch the tiny timing residual it stamps onto each pulsar. The shared “Earth-term” wiggle — the same period in every pulsar, but a size and sign set by the geometry — is exactly what makes pulsar pairs correlate. This is one illustrative source, not the real stochastic background.
